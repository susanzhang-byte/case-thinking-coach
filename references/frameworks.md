# Framework construction guide

Frameworks are starting vocabulary, not scripts. Begin with the decision and success metric. Select the components that would change the recommendation, build 3–5 case-specific branches, and identify where to start.

## Five foundational named frameworks

### 1. Profitability

**Use when:** profit, margin, revenue, or cost performance has declined—or when a proposed action must improve profit.

**Core structure:**

- `Profit = Revenue − Costs`
- `Revenue = Price × Volume`; segment price and volume by product, customer, geography, or channel when useful.
- `Costs = Fixed costs + Variable costs`; distinguish changes in unit cost, volume, capacity, and overhead.

**Case use:** locate the changed branch, identify the root cause, quantify its contribution to the gap, and test feasible remedies.

**Limitation:** the equation diagnoses financial symptoms but does not by itself explain customer behavior, competitive dynamics, capabilities, or implementation.

### 2. The 3 Cs: Company, Customers, Competitors

**Use when:** entering a market, launching a product, assessing growth, or forming a broad strategic view.

**Core structure:**

- **Company:** objectives, economics, brand, capabilities, channels, resources, and constraints.
- **Customers:** segments, needs, purchase criteria, willingness to pay, behavior, and unmet demand.
- **Competitors:** share, positioning, economics, advantages, likely reactions, and substitutes.

**Limitation:** it can become a descriptive inventory. Tie every observation to the client decision and add explicit economics and implementation where needed.

### 3. Porter’s Five Forces

**Use when:** assessing industry structure, market attractiveness, or the durability of industry profit pools.

**Core structure:**

- rivalry among existing competitors;
- supplier bargaining power;
- buyer bargaining power;
- threat of substitutes;
- threat of new entrants.

**Limitation:** it analyzes industry structure, not whether this specific company can win. Use only the material forces and combine them with company capabilities and economics.

### 4. The 4 Ps: Product, Price, Place, Promotion

**Use when:** developing marketing strategy, launching a product, or positioning/repositioning a brand.

**Core structure:**

- **Product:** customer problem, features, differentiation, proof, fit, and lifecycle.
- **Price:** willingness to pay, value perception, costs, competitive pricing, and price architecture.
- **Place:** sales and distribution channels, availability, channel economics, and customer journey.
- **Promotion:** message, creative, media/channel mix, funnel, and measurement.

**Limitation:** it can underweight market attractiveness, company capabilities, unit economics, and execution risks. Add them when they affect the decision.

### 5. Mergers and acquisitions (M&A)

**Use when:** deciding whether a client should acquire, merge with, invest in, or partner with another company. Keep cases at a business-strategy level rather than technical investment-banking execution.

**Core structure:**

- **Strategic rationale:** growth, market access, capabilities, defensive logic, and build/buy/partner alternatives.
- **Target and market attractiveness:** customers, competition, industry outlook, and target advantages.
- **Financial viability:** standalone performance, purchase price/valuation assumptions, revenue and cost synergies, funding capacity, payback, and downside scenarios.
- **Due diligence and risks:** quality of earnings, customers, operations, regulation at a non-legal level, culture, systems, and integration feasibility.
- **Integration and value capture:** operating model, owners, timing, costs, milestones, and protections against customer or talent loss.

**Limitation:** strategic fit does not prove that the price is attractive or that synergies can be captured. Keep assumptions explicit and test a no-deal alternative.

## Situation-specific building blocks

- **GTM:** need; proposition; economics; channel/launch plan; capabilities and risks.
- **Pricing:** objective; customer value; alternatives; unit economics/volume; implementation.
- **Brand:** current perception; target customer; competitive frame; promise/proof; activation and measurement.
- **Growth:** baseline; core penetration; new customers/products/geographies/channels; economics; risks.
- **Marketing ROI:** objective/baseline; audience/funnel; incremental impact; economics; allocation/experimentation.
- **Operations/turnaround:** performance gap; demand/service; process/capacity; cost/productivity; implementation sequence.
- **Communications:** objective; stakeholders; perception; message/proof; channel/timing; measurement.
- **Safe crisis:** verify; stakeholder impact; immediate containment; message/messenger; cadence; recovery.

## Tailoring rule

A strong answer rarely recites a named framework. For a market-entry case, for example, combine relevant industry forces, customer demand, company fit, economics, entry options, and risks into one decision-specific issue tree. A stakeholder map, funnel, timeline, or system model may outperform a strict MECE tree. Penalize overlap only when it causes confusion or double-counting.
